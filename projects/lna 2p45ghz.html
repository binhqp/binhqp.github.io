2.45 GHz low-noise amplifier on FR4

I took a low-noise amplifier for the 2.4 GHz band from transistor biasing through electromagnetic simulation and PCB layout to a manufactured, measured board.

Context
Project High-Frequency Amplifier Design, Ulm University, summer semester 2022
Transistor
Infineon BFR840L3RHESD
Board
FR4, 0.8 mm, grounded coplanar waveguide feed lines
Tools
Keysight ADS for circuit and EM simulation, KiCad for layout
Result
Gain above 12 dB over 700 MHz, IP1dB of −15 dBm

Goal and requirements

The task was to design and build an amplifier that boosts weak signals around 2.45 GHz while adding as little noise as possible, then check the real board against the simulation. The design followed a fixed order: bias, stabilization, matching, layout, simulation, fabrication and measurement.

RequirementTargetResult
Gain> 10 dB> 12 dB over 700 MHz (measured)
Reflection< −10 dBS11 below −10 dB over 500 MHz, S22 over 600 MHz (measured)
Noise figure< 2 dBAbout 1.3 dB at 2.45 GHz (simulated)

Design

  1. Choose the bias point

    Sweeping the base voltage showed the lowest minimum noise figure, 0.75 dB, at about 0.8 V. The collector voltage hardly changed noise or gain between 1.2 V and 3.6 V, so I set the base to 0.8 V and the collector to 1.8 V.

  2. Stabilize the transistor

    The transistor is fast (transition frequency of 70 GHz), so I checked stability over a very wide range. The bare device was potentially unstable from 33 MHz to 9 GHz. A resistive network on the collector, a 27 Ω resistor behind a capacitor, raised the stability factor K above 1.

  3. Match input and output

    Noise and gain want different source impedances, so the input match is a compromise: a minimum noise figure of 1.2 dB with a maximum available gain of 16.5 dB. The bias feeds join the matching networks, and each supply rail uses four capacitors in parallel (2.2 pF to 2.2 µF) to form an RF ground over a wide frequency range.

  4. Lay out and simulate electromagnetically

    I drew the board in KiCad and imported it into ADS, so the transmission lines, pads and vias were simulated electromagnetically and combined with the transistor and capacitor models in one co-simulation.

  5. Fabricate and measure

    The board was manufactured, assembled and measured for S-parameters and for gain versus input power.

Measurement

The measured S-parameters follow the co-simulation closely. The reflection dip and the gain peak sit slightly lower in frequency than predicted, and the measured gain is a few tenths of a decibel below the simulation. The dashed curves show the simulation and the solid curves the measurement.

A power sweep shows the same trend as the simulation. Gain starts to compress as the input power rises, and the 1 dB compression point lies at an input power of −15 dBm, above the −20 dBm target.

An open question: spurious tones

The measurements also showed spurious tones at 1.1 GHz and 1.35 GHz, and the DC collector current jumped as the input power changed. To test whether the amplifier was oscillating, I rechecked stability in simulation and in the measured S-parameters. The minimum K factor was 1.15 at 13.5 GHz, and the stability measures μ and μ′ stayed above 1, so neither showed an instability at those frequencies.

I also refined the mesh and widened the frequency plan of the electromagnetic simulation, but it did not explain the tones. Their cause is still open. The amplifier meets its specification, and this part of the project showed me where a simulation and a real board can still disagree.

What I took away

  • The full RF design loop on one board: bias, stability, matching, layout, simulation and measurement.
  • Low noise and high gain compete at the input match, so the match is always a compromise.
  • Electromagnetic simulation of the real layout catches effects that an ideal schematic hides.
  • Passing every simulated stability check does not guarantee clean behavior on the bench, so measurement is the real test.
